On July 28, 1976, a U.S. Air Force SR-71 Blackbird crew flew 2,193 mph, the absolute speed record for a jet. Fifty years later, it still stands. Here's the math, the asterisk, and why nothing has beaten it.

Key numbers
• 2,193.167 mph: absolute speed record, July 28, 1976, Capt. Eldon Joersz (pilot) and Maj. George Morgan (RSO) (National Museum of the USAF; U.S. Air Force)
• Mach 3.3: the SR-71's maximum safe operating speed at about 85,000 ft (Smithsonian NASM)
• ~600°F: heat on the outer skin above Mach 3, which is why the airframe was built largely of titanium (Smithsonian NASM)
• 1 h 4 min 20 s: Los Angeles to Washington on March 6, 1990, the Smithsonian SR-71's final Air Force flight, averaging about 2,124 mph (Smithsonian NASM)
• 32 Blackbirds built (Smithsonian NASM)
• F-35A listed top speed: Mach 1.6 (~1,200 mph) (U.S. Air Force fact sheet)
• X-15: 4,520 mph (Mach 6.7) on Oct. 3, 1967 — rocket-powered and air-launched from a B-52 (NASA; Air Force Test Center)

Our math (Apex Reports)
• 3,600 seconds ÷ 2,193 mph ≈ 1.64 seconds per mile
• 2,193 ÷ ~1,200 mph ≈ 1.8x the F-35A's listed top speed ("almost twice")

Caveats: The record is for jet (air-breathing) aircraft that take off under their own power; the rocket-powered X-15 flew faster but was launched from a B-52. "Officially" means a ratified record — other aircraft may have flown fast at times without setting one. The Air Force retired its SR-71 fleet on Jan. 26, 1990, citing a shrinking defense budget and high operating costs (National Museum of the USAF); spy satellites were the other big reason (The National Interest; MiGFlug). NASA flew SR-71s until 1999. Figures are rounded.
